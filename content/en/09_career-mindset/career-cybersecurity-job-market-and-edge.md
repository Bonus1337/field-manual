# Cybersecurity Did Not Die. The Time for Chaotic Profiles Just Ended.

## Why I am writing this down at all

Because I feel like it is ridiculously easy today to destroy your own perspective with extreme narratives.

On one side, you hear everywhere that cybersecurity is the future, that there is a talent shortage, that the market is growing, that now is the time to get in.
On the other side, you just as often see people saying the whole market is dead, nobody wants juniors, requirements are absurd, and without experience you should not even bother.

And the truth is both sides are touching part of reality, but neither of them says the whole thing.

Cybersecurity did not die.
It also did not suddenly become easy.
What happened is much simpler:
the time when it was enough to _sound_ like someone from the industry is over.

Today, what matters much more is whether you are clear.
Whether people understand what you are about.
Whether you can show something beyond enthusiasm.
Whether you can carry a real part of a real problem.
Because a company does not hire me because I am “interested in security.”
A company hires me because I am cheaper than the risk I can help reduce.

And honestly, that is probably the healthiest place to start.

---

## The market is not dead. The market is just ruthless toward mediocrity.

This is probably the single best sentence to burn into your head.

Because there is still a lot of work in security.
Incidents are not going away.
Chaos is not shrinking.
Regulations keep growing.
Artificial intelligence did not calm anything down - in many places it just poured fuel on the fire.
Companies still have a mess in processes, systems, permissions, configurations, and ownership.

So no - the problem is not that security stopped being needed.

The problem is that companies are far less willing to hire people who are vague, broad, blurry, and hard to understand.

This is not a cold market.
It is a hot market with a much harsher filter.

Exactly like this:
the opportunities exist, but not for everyone.
The demand exists, but not for just anyone.
There is a shortage, but not of random people.

And that paradox looks strange only at first.
In reality it makes perfect sense.
If a company has a problem that costs time, money, audit pressure, reputation, or management peace of mind, it does not want to hire another person who is still a problem to solve.
It wants someone who reduces uncertainty instead of adding more of it.

---

## The biggest mistake is thinking that enthusiasm alone gets you anywhere

Enthusiasm is great.
Without it, it is hard to stay in this space long enough to become good.
But enthusiasm is fuel.
It is not the product.

The fact that I spend nights in labs, write write-ups, dig through web applications, analyze mistakes, read about vulnerabilities, and genuinely enjoy exploitation - that is a good start.
But from the market’s perspective, that alone is still not value.

It becomes valuable only when:

- I understand the system, not just the tool,
- I can close things out,
- I can show my work,
- I can explain what I did,
- I can translate technical chaos into a real problem,
- I can be useful.

That is the difference between someone who “likes cyber” and someone you can actually trust with something.

And the sooner a person stops building their identity around “I am passionate about security,” the better.
Because at some point you have to stop sounding like a fan of the field and start looking like someone who can actually deliver inside it.

---

## I am seeing more and more that cybersecurity is usually a next step, not a first one

This is one of those thoughts that really organizes everything.

Security by itself is rarely the strongest first foundation.
Much more often, it is built on top of something you already understood before.

Systems administration, networking, help desk, infrastructure, development, cloud, operations, process, compliance, risk - all of those can become very solid entry points into security.

And that makes sense.
It is much easier to secure something you have actually worked with before.
It is much easier to test an application when you understand how software gets built.
It is much easier to move into cloud security when cloud is not just some magical black box to you.
It is much easier to move into governance, risk, and compliance when you understand process, documentation, requirements, and organizational chaos.

The longer I look at this, the less I believe in the romantic idea of entering cybersecurity purely by consuming cybersecurity content.

You can get in.
But much less often can you just jump in.
Most of the time you have to build a bridge.

And for me that leads to a better question than just “how do I get into cyber?”:

**what foundation am I building so that my move into cybersecurity looks credible to the market?**

---

## A certificate alone does not make anyone a specialist. But the lack of a signal does not help either.

It is very easy to fall into extremes here too.

Some people say certifications are just pieces of paper and mean nothing.
Others treat them like magic keys.

The truth is much simpler:
a certification helps, but it does not carry the person by itself.

It helps with the first filter.
It helps structure learning.
It helps signal direction to the market.
It helps show that you are not moving randomly.

But if nothing stands behind it, it starts looking weak very quickly.

Because if someone has a profile full of certifications but no repository, no write-up, no project, no meaningful analysis, no visible trace of work, it often starts looking less impressive, not more.

When somebody seems to have everything but shows nothing, one question appears fast:
okay, but where is the practice?

That is why this mindset makes much more sense to me:

**a certification should strengthen a meaningful path, not cover up the lack of experience.**

Direction first.
Then proof of work.
Then the certificate as reinforcement.
Not the other way around.

---

## A “bit of everything” profile sounds ambitious, but it often loses in the end

This is probably one of the most common traps for people who genuinely want to go deep.

Because cybersecurity is wide.
Too wide.
And everything looks interesting.

A bit of penetration testing.
A bit of cloud.
A bit of security operations center work.
A bit of open-source intelligence.
A bit of application security.
A bit of development security operations.
A bit of artificial intelligence.
A bit of networking.
A bit of Linux.
A bit of automation.

It sounds good.
But over time it often turns into a profile that nobody can quickly understand.

And the market does not like guessing.
A recruiter will not sit there trying to figure out what your core really is.
A manager will not assemble your identity for you like a puzzle.
They want to know whether you fit the problem sitting on their desk.

That is exactly why specialization creates an edge.
Not because everything else stops existing.
But because someone has to be able to describe you in one clear sentence.

Not:
“knows a bit of this and a bit of that.”
But:
“this is the person for this area.”

That does not mean you have to lock yourself into one box forever.
But at a certain point you have to stop looking like a list of buzzwords.

Because the brutal truth is this:
**if you seem to do everything, the market will often assume you do nothing well enough.**

---

## Today, more than before, what matters is what can actually be seen

This hits me personally quite hard.

Because you can sit alone for months.
Do labs.
Study.
Make mistakes.
Fix things.
Write notes.
Build folders full of materials.
And nobody outside of you knows any of that exists.

The market does not see what you never show.

It does not see your bookmarks.
It does not see your private notes.
It does not see the hours.
It does not see the frustration.
It does not see the attempts.
It does not see the fact that you are genuinely deep in the subject.

What becomes visible is only the trace.

A post.
A write-up.
A repository.
A small project.
An analysis.
A short note.
A comment that adds something useful.
A breakdown of a mistake and how you figured it out.
Consistency.

And in my opinion this is exactly where a huge edge can be built today.

Because if you do not have major commercial experience yet, you can still have something that shows how you think.
And that already means a lot.

In a way, it even changes the mechanics of recruiting.
Because when you regularly show meaningful work, you are no longer just someone who “sends a curriculum vitae.”
You become a person somebody may already know before you even click apply.

And that is powerful.
Very powerful.

---

## LinkedIn, GitHub, write-ups, and public notes are not a side thing anymore. They are part of the work.

There was a time when you could treat all of that as optional.
Today, less and less.

Not because everybody has to chase algorithms.
But because a visible public trace of work reduces uncertainty for the other side.

If you regularly show:

- what you built,
- what you learned,
- what failed,
- how your thinking improved,
- how you analyze problems,

then people do not just see that you are “learning.”
They see **how** you learn.

And that makes a huge difference.

Because somebody can have a polished curriculum vitae and no depth.
And somebody else can have a simple profile, but behind it there is a real, consistent trail of work.
I think we both know which one stays in people’s minds longer.

This matters to me personally too.
Because the longer I do this, the more I see that publishing does not have to be “marketing.”
It can be a very practical part of building your own position.

Not everything has to be huge.
Not everything has to be perfect.
Not everything has to look like a polished professional publication.

But it should be real.
It should carry weight.
It should leave behind a signal that you are not just another person saying they are “trying to get into the field.”

---

## Companies are no longer buying passion alone. They are buying autonomy, structure, and the ability to handle chaos.

This is one of the strongest conclusions.

A company does not hire just to have a nice enthusiastic person around.
A company hires to have less mess, less risk, less guesswork, and fewer problems.

That is why the following matter so much:

- autonomy,
- system-level understanding,
- prioritization,
- structure in work,
- documentation,
- reporting,
- closing things properly,
- tolerance for chaos,
- communication.

These things do not sound as exciting as exploits, red teaming, or custom tooling.
But they are often exactly what separates somebody who looks impressive at first from somebody who actually delivers.

The longer I look at this, the more I see that being organized and dependable is also a real skill.
And it is one of those skills you cannot fake for very long.

---

## Communication is not a soft extra. In security, it is also a weapon.

This is something a lot of technical people try to push aside.
As if the technical quality should defend itself.

It does not.
At least not always.

Because even if you understand the problem perfectly, you still need to make somebody else understand:

- what is wrong,
- why it matters,
- what risk it creates,
- what needs to be done,
- what happens if nobody acts.

And this is not about becoming some overtalking sales person.
It is about being someone who can move a thought clearly from their own head into somebody else’s.

That has massive value in security.
A report without a clear narrative is weaker.
A strong analysis without a clear message loses half its force.
A good curriculum vitae without a coherent signal becomes blurred.
Strong knowledge without the ability to communicate never works at full strength.

That is uncomfortable for people who prefer staying inside the technical layer and not stepping out toward people.
I understand that myself.
But it does not make it any less true.

---

## Tools will change faster than ever. What remains is the way you think.

This also clears up the picture a lot.

Today it is very easy to attach yourself to tools, vendors, trendy stacks, and new hype.
But all of that will change so fast that building your whole identity around it is short-sighted.

What matters much more is understanding:

- why a given class of tools exists,
- what problem it solves,
- where its limits are,
- how the system logic works,
- what is the goal and what is only the means.

It is similar to offensive security.
Payloads change.
Environments change.
Tooling changes.
But if you understand the mechanics of the problem, you do not start from zero every time.

And it is exactly the same here.
It is not worth trying to memorize everything.
It is worth understanding why it exists in the first place.

---

## Artificial intelligence is not killing this field. Artificial intelligence is killing mediocrity.

This is a thought I really want to keep.

Artificial intelligence speeds things up.
Automates.
Helps structure parts of the work.
Helps create faster, analyze faster, scale faster.
And yes, it also helps attackers.

But artificial intelligence does not make human specialists irrelevant.
What it really does is make average pattern repetition less valuable.

The people becoming more valuable are the ones who can:

- understand context,
- verify results,
- connect facts,
- notice what is off,
- recognize that something “formally looks fine” but practically smells wrong,
- make decisions.

So once again:
the tool itself is not the edge.
The edge is the quality of the person using it.

---

## There are directions that genuinely look strong right now

This is not about chasing trends just because they are trends.
It is about seeing where the market’s real pain is accumulating.

For me, the strongest areas look like these:

### Cloud Security

Because cloud has been growing for years and is not going anywhere.
And where growth is fast, the mess usually grows fast too.
Identity and access management, configuration, visibility of resources, permissions, governance - these are not the most glamorous topics in the world, but they are deeply needed.

### Application Security / Development Security Operations

Because if software production keeps accelerating, someone has to make sure vulnerabilities are not being produced faster than features.
Especially now, when more and more things are built in a “just ship it faster” mode.

### Incident Response / Security Operations Center

Because attacks have not slowed down.
If anything, they have accelerated.
Automation on the attacker side only adds pressure.
Somebody has to understand that mess, filter it, and handle it.

### Governance, Risk, and Compliance / regulations

Because for many companies, security is no longer a nice extra.
It is an obligation.
And that creates real demand for people who understand requirements, process, order, audit, and how to turn all of that into actual practice.

### Artificial Intelligence / Large Language Model Security

Because deploying a model is easier than deploying it well.
And that area is only going to grow.

This does not mean everything else stopped mattering.
It just means it is worth seeing where the market is actually feeling pain.

---

## Money does not follow the title alone. It follows responsibility and scarcity.

This is worth repeating too, because the internet loves shortcuts.

Big money does not come from having a trendy headline.
It does not come from sounding “industry-like.”
It does not come from looking polished in a banner.

It comes from being able to do difficult things.
From carrying real responsibility.
From handling problems that other people either cannot solve or do not want to touch.
From creating real impact.

That is why specialization, weight, and consistent delivery matter so much.
Because in the end, the market does not pay for declarations.
It pays for the ability to carry a hard problem.

---

## The most important thing I want to leave myself with

If I had to reduce all of this to one sentence, it would probably be this:

**I do not want to build the profile of someone who “wants to get into cybersecurity.” I want to build the profile of someone who can be trusted with a real problem and not make a mess of it.**

That changes the perspective on everything.

On learning.
On certifications.
On publishing.
On the curriculum vitae.
On choosing a specialization.
On interviews.
On personal growth.

Because then you stop asking:
“how do I look like someone from the field?”
and start asking:
**“what do I need to do to become genuinely useful to this field?”**

And honestly, that is probably the most truthful summary of the whole thing:

Cybersecurity still offers huge opportunities.
But it tolerates chaos, vagueness, and pretending less and less.

This is no longer the moment for collecting buzzwords.
This is not the moment for being “a little bit of everything.”
This is not the moment for hiding your work in a drawer.
This is not the moment for believing enthusiasm alone will carry you.

This is the moment to build weight.
To build clarity.
To do work that leaves a trace.
To show that behind the interest there is actual execution.

And honestly?
That is not bad news at all.

Because if you are really doing the work, learning consciously, showing your process, and building something coherent, you can still turn this into a very strong path.

Just not through shortcuts.
And that is exactly how it should be.
