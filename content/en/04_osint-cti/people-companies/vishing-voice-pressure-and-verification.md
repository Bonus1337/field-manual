# Vishing - when a voice takes control of the conversation

Vishing is phishing transferred into a telephone conversation.

That does not mean it is simply the voice-based version of a fake email.

The telephone changes the entire dynamics of the attack.

An email can be read again, shown to a colleague, checked for sender details and revisited after several minutes. A phone call is happening now. The caller reacts to every hesitation, adjusts the story, answers questions and maintains pressure.

The victim is not analyzing a finished piece of text.

They are participating in a situation being created in real time.

The attacker may impersonate a bank employee, IT support, a government office, a service provider, a manager or a family member.

They do not need to ask for a password immediately.

They may begin with information designed to create anxiety:

> We have detected a suspicious transaction.

> Someone is trying to access your account.

> We have identified a security issue on your computer.

> Your relative has been involved in an accident.

> We urgently need to confirm a change to your details.

A solution then appears.

The problem is that the solution is controlled by the same person who created the crisis moments earlier.

---

## Guiding rule: the caller cannot be both the source of the threat and the only way to safety

The strongest vishing scenarios use a simple structure:

**threat → tension → assistance → action**

The caller describes a danger and then presents themselves as the person capable of stopping it.

The victim has no time for an independent assessment.

To protect their money, account, device or loved one, they are supposedly required to follow the instructions immediately.

At that point, the caller takes control over how the situation is interpreted.

They decide:

- what has happened,
- how serious the risk is,
- why time matters,
- what the victim must not do,
- whom the victim should not trust,
- which action will solve the problem.

A safe response begins by taking that control away.

There is no need to prove during the call that the person is a fraudster.

It is enough to end the conversation and independently contact the organization through a known channel.

---

## Vishing is a process, not a single request

A well-prepared telephone attack consists of several connected elements:

**reconnaissance → role selection → emotional trigger → authority building → isolation → request → escalation**

The attacker may already know:

- where the victim works,
- the name of their manager,
- which bank they use,
- which systems the organization relies on,
- who is responsible for technical support,
- which events are currently taking place,
- which supplier provides a particular service.

They do not need to know everything.

A few accurate details may be enough to make the rest of the story believable.

Someone who knows the correct department name, a manager’s name and the name of an internal application may be treated as an employee.

Real information becomes the glue holding a false identity together.

---

## Voice allows the attacker to control the pace

A written message is static.

A conversation is alive.

The attacker can hear:

- uncertainty,
- nervousness,
- lack of knowledge,
- willingness to help,
- resistance,
- curiosity,
- irritation.

They adapt accordingly.

When the victim becomes frightened, the attacker increases pressure.

When resistance appears, they invoke authority.

When the target does not understand the terminology, the attacker may use even more technical language.

When the person wants to end the call, a warning appears:

> Please do not hang up, because we will no longer be able to secure your account.

The objective is to keep the victim inside the attacker’s narrative.

As long as the conversation continues, the attacker can influence each new decision.

---

## Reconnaissance: choose a role the target will hesitate to challenge

Vishing becomes much more effective when the role fits the target’s environment.

Inside an organization, the attacker may pretend to be:

- a service desk employee,
- an administrator,
- a security specialist,
- an auditor,
- a system vendor,
- an HR employee,
- someone from accounting,
- a manager from another branch.

When targeting a private person, they may impersonate:

- a bank,
- the police,
- a telecommunications provider,
- a courier,
- a government office,
- an energy supplier,
- a family member,
- a doctor or hospital employee.

The attacker chooses a role that appears to have a natural right to ask questions or issue instructions.

That does not mean the caller actually has such authority.

It means the story fits the social image of the institution.

An employee does not know everyone in IT.

A customer does not know every bank consultant.

An elderly person may not know how a government office normally communicates.

That uncertainty benefits the attacker.

---

## Authority does not require the highest position

The attacker does not always need to impersonate a CEO.

A role associated with competence in a specific area may be more effective.

A service desk employee may ask the user to perform actions on a workstation.

A bank consultant may ask about a suspicious transaction.

An administrator may claim that an account requires reconfiguration.

An auditor may refer to a compliance review.

Authority can be created through:

- specialist vocabulary,
- confidence,
- knowledge of system names,
- references to procedures,
- references to managers,
- information obtained through OSINT,
- familiarity with real events inside the organization.

The target may begin to think:

> They know more than I do, so I should follow their instructions.

That difference in perceived knowledge becomes a tool of influence.

---

## Complex language may be used to create confusion deliberately

The attacker may use terms that the victim does not understand.

Technical accuracy is not always the main objective.

The purpose may be to create the impression that the issue is too complicated for the target to evaluate independently.

The employee hears about:

- synchronization errors,
- token lockouts,
- configuration conflicts,
- invalid sessions,
- reauthentication requirements,
- host problems,
- security-policy changes.

They do not want to appear incompetent.

Instead of questioning the request, they may confirm information and follow instructions.

A secure process should give every employee permission to say:

> I do not understand this request. I will verify it with IT through the standard channel.

A lack of technical knowledge should never remove someone’s right to stop the process.

---

## Time pressure limits the victim’s ability to regain control

The attacker does not want the victim to:

- ask a colleague,
- check the procedure,
- call the bank,
- contact a manager,
- verify the information in an application,
- calmly consider the situation.

That is why urgency appears.

> The transaction is being processed right now.

> The account will be blocked within minutes.

> The incident is already spreading.

> The manager is waiting for confirmation.

> Please do not hang up.

Pressure does not always sound threatening.

It may be presented as concern:

> I want to help you, but we need to act immediately.

The objective remains the same.

The victim must act before regaining distance from the situation.

---

## Keeping the call active is part of the attack

A request not to hang up should be treated as a serious warning sign.

Ending the call gives the victim several advantages:

- silence,
- time,
- an opportunity to consult someone,
- the ability to initiate a new call independently,
- control over the communication channel,
- relief from emotional pressure.

The attacker loses the ability to react immediately.

They can no longer explain every inconsistency or interfere with verification.

One of the most important defensive habits is therefore:

> I end the call and initiate contact with the organization myself.

Do not call back through the recent-calls list.

Do not use a number provided by the caller.

Use the number from the official application, website, bank card or internal directory.

---

## The number displayed on the screen does not prove identity

Caller ID should not be treated as an authentication mechanism.

The number may look correct, already be saved in contacts or resemble an official helpline.

That does not mean the call genuinely originates from that person or institution.

Attackers may abuse internet telephony and weaknesses in telecommunications trust mechanisms to change the number shown to the recipient.

The phone may display:

- the bank’s number,
- the company’s number,
- an employee’s number,
- a government number,
- the name of a saved contact.

It is only information displayed by the device.

It is not proof of who is calling.

A useful defensive rule is:

> The number may help identify the call, but it must never authorize a sensitive action by itself.

---

## Deep voice changes the meaning of a familiar voice

For a long time, recognizing the voice of a relative or colleague was treated as strong confirmation of identity.

Voice-synthesis technology weakens that assumption.

Public recordings from social media, podcasts, online meetings or videos may provide enough material to imitate someone’s voice.

The attack does not need to support a complete and natural conversation.

It may use:

- prerecorded statements,
- short generated responses,
- voice messages,
- fragments of conversation,
- generated instructions,
- an excuse involving poor call quality.

Background noise, urgency or supposed emotional distress can explain imperfections in the voice.

Recognizing the tone should therefore not end verification in a sensitive situation.

---

## A private question may be stronger than voice recognition

When an unusual request appears to come from a loved one, it is useful to rely on information that is not publicly available.

This may include:

- a shared experience,
- a private event,
- a previously agreed phrase,
- a family password,
- a detail known only to both people.

The question should be difficult to answer using information from social media.

A weak question:

> What is your dog’s name?

The answer may be visible on the person’s profile.

A stronger question:

> What happened the last time we travelled home together?

This does not replace all other verification methods, but it may interrupt a scenario based on a synthesized voice.

---

## Strong emotion limits access to rational analysis

Vishing often relies on events that trigger an immediate stress response.

The scenario may involve:

- losing money,
- a loved one being in danger,
- an accusation of criminal activity,
- the risk of losing a job,
- an account lockout,
- responsibility for an incident,
- possible public embarrassment.

Under intense stress, the victim begins looking for the fastest way to restore safety.

This does not mean they stop thinking.

It means their thinking becomes focused on resolving the immediate crisis.

The attacker provides a ready-made path:

> Transfer the money to a safe account.

> Provide the authorization code.

> Install this application.

> Give me the session ID.

> Confirm the operation.

The victim is no longer evaluating whether the request is logical.

They are trying to end the emergency.

---

## Emotional contrast increases susceptibility to influence

First comes a strong negative stimulus.

Then the caller offers relief.

> Someone is stealing your money, but I can stop it.

> Your relative is in trouble, but paying the deposit will help.

> Your account will be blocked, but it can be secured immediately.

The transition from fear to hope may increase willingness to cooperate.

The victim begins to see the caller not as the source of information, but as the person saving the situation.

Refusing the caller’s request may then feel risky.

The defensive procedure should therefore be automatic:

> When someone asks for data, payment or software installation, I end the call.

---

## Empathy can also be weaponized

The attacker does not always threaten.

They may ask for help.

> I still have several other people to call.

> If we do this now, I can close the ticket more quickly.

> Please help me, otherwise I will have to escalate the whole case.

> The manager is waiting for the result.

The employee may cooperate not because they believe in the authority, but because they do not want to make another person’s work more difficult.

In organizations where helpfulness is strongly valued, this may be especially effective.

The principle remains simple:

> Helping another person cannot require bypassing a security control.

---

## Remote access is one of the most dangerous points in the conversation

A common objective of vishing is to persuade the victim to install or launch remote-administration software.

The caller may claim it is needed to:

- repair the computer,
- secure a bank account,
- investigate an application problem,
- remove malware,
- configure the system,
- provide technical support.

After receiving the session identifier and access code, the attacker may take control of the device.

When the user is already signed in to email, banking or corporate systems, remote access may bypass many earlier controls.

The attacker uses the legitimate user’s active session.

Neither employees nor customers should install software recommended by someone who initiated an unexpected call.

---

## Legitimate technical support should be recognizable

An organization cannot expect employees to recognize fake support when the real support process is chaotic.

Employees should know:

- how the IT team initiates contact,
- whether unannounced telephone calls are permitted,
- what information a legitimate technician can provide,
- what a technician will never request,
- whether a ticket number is required,
- how to verify the technician’s identity,
- what the remote-support process looks like,
- which tools are approved.

When every administrator works differently, the attacker can imitate almost any behavior.

A predictable process is a security control.

---

## A request for a password should end the call

No support process should require someone to disclose a password by telephone.

The same applies to:

- one-time codes,
- BLIK payment codes,
- MFA codes,
- PINs,
- complete card details,
- email passwords,
- recovery codes,
- online-banking credentials.

The attacker may rename the information:

> It is not a password, only a verification code.

> I need the security identifier.

> It is just a session confirmation.

The technical meaning does not change.

When a value can authorize a login, payment or access request, it should not be shared with the caller.

---

## The attacker may move the boundary through small steps

Vishing often begins with harmless questions.

> Am I speaking with Anna?

> Are you currently using your work laptop?

> Can you see the application icon?

> Could you open the settings?

> Please download the tool now.

> Please provide the code.

Each request follows naturally from the previous one.

It becomes harder to stop because the victim is already involved.

They may think:

> I have already completed several steps, so this must be legitimate.

From a defensive perspective, every new action requires a separate assessment.

Previous cooperation does not authorize the next request.

---

## Refusal does not necessarily end the attack

A prepared caller expects resistance.

They may answer:

> I understand your caution, but this is the standard process.

> I will transfer you to my manager.

> Please check the number. I am calling from headquarters.

> I have the ticket number.

> A lack of cooperation may lead to an account lockout.

> Your manager has already been informed.

The attacker tries to turn caution into the problem.

The victim is made to feel that they are obstructing a legitimate process.

The safest response is not to continue arguing.

It is to end the call.

The longer the conversation continues, the more opportunities the attacker has to influence the target.

---

## An internal sense of control helps interrupt the narrative

A telephone attack may create the impression that everything is happening beyond the victim’s control.

Someone knows their details.

Someone claims the account is under attack.

Someone insists that immediate action is required.

At that moment, it is worth returning to the things that remain under our control.

We can:

- end the call,
- refuse to provide information,
- avoid installing software,
- call the organization independently,
- consult another person,
- block the account,
- report the number,
- ask for help.

The most important defensive mechanism is not perfect fraud detection.

It is understanding that the process can always be interrupted.

---

## You do not need certainty to end the call

A common mistake is trying to prove that the caller is a fraudster.

The victim begins asking questions, analyzing the accent, checking the number and judging the responses.

Meanwhile, the conversation continues.

The attacker still has the ability to manipulate the situation.

Absolute certainty is unnecessary.

It is enough to recognize:

> This conversation concerns a sensitive operation and was initiated by the other party.

That alone is a sufficient reason to end the call and contact the institution independently.

A legitimate organization should accept this method of verification.

---

## Email confirmation may help, but it does not solve everything

In a corporate environment, the caller may be asked to confirm the request by email.

This creates a record and enables further review.

The email should not automatically be treated as proof of identity.

The attacker may:

- use a similar-looking domain,
- spoof the sender,
- use a compromised account,
- send from an external service,
- already have access to the mailbox.

The strongest process uses an independent channel and a known contact path.

For example, the employee may create a ticket in the internal system or call the service desk using the company directory.

---

## An official application can confirm the consultant

One useful approach is to confirm the call through the institution’s official application.

A customer receives a telephone call and then sees information inside the banking application showing:

- that contact is taking place,
- who is conducting the conversation,
- which department they represent,
- why the institution is calling.

The verification no longer depends on the number shown by the telephone.

It relies on a separate, authenticated channel.

This significantly increases the difficulty of impersonating an institution.

A similar model can be used internally through an employee portal, ticketing platform or corporate messenger.

---

## A vishing assessment should test the process, not acting ability

The purpose of the assessment should not be to prove that the tester can perform a convincing role.

It should test a specific control.

Example hypotheses:

> Employees install remote-support software based on an unverified telephone request.

> The service desk resets passwords without adequately verifying the caller.

> Employees disclose information about internal systems to someone claiming to be an administrator.

> Financial details can be changed based solely on a telephone conversation.

The hypothesis defines when the assessment should end.

When the employee begins downloading an unapproved application, the weakness may already be sufficiently demonstrated.

Taking control of the device may be unnecessary.

---

## The testing pretext must be proportionate

A scenario should be realistic without causing unnecessary emotional harm.

Special care is needed with stories involving:

- an accident affecting a loved one,
- death,
- illness,
- job loss,
- criminal proceedings,
- loss of substantial funds,
- threats to children,
- violence.

Such pretexts may cause intense stress that is unnecessary for testing most organizational controls.

A technical, administrative or process-related scenario is often sufficient in a business environment.

Realism does not mean the absence of limits.

---

## Minimum evidence should limit impact

When the objective is to determine whether an employee would provide a remote-session code, it may not be necessary to connect to the device.

Evidence may include:

- consent to install the application,
- launching the tool,
- willingness to provide the code,
- disclosure of the session identifier,
- an attempt to follow the instruction.

When a connection is required, it should lead only to a safe and agreed action.

The tester should not:

- browse real documents,
- copy data,
- establish persistence,
- disable protections,
- install uncontrolled software,
- exceed the purpose of the campaign.

The assessment ends after sufficient evidence has been obtained.

---

## Recording calls requires clear rules

Campaign documentation may include recordings, notes or transcripts.

Before the assessment begins, define:

- whether calls will be recorded,
- who may access the recordings,
- how long they will be stored,
- whether employee voices will be anonymized,
- how personal data will be protected,
- whether the report will include direct quotes,
- who may listen to the material.

A complete recording may contain far more information than the report requires.

Data minimization also applies to test documentation.

---

## The campaign should not measure only who complied

Useful metrics may include:

- how many people ended the call,
- how many attempted independent verification,
- time to the first report,
- service-desk response,
- escalation to a manager,
- blocking the number,
- warning other employees,
- correct use of the procedure,
- amount of information disclosed,
- the stage at which the attack was stopped.

Someone who initially followed one instruction but then ended the call and reported the incident behaved differently from someone who gave full access.

The report should measure the resilience of the entire system.

---

## Reporting should be easier than continuing the conversation

Employees need to know what to do after a suspicious call.

They should not have to search for the correct form or decide whether the situation is serious enough.

The process may include:

- a simple service-desk number,
- a corporate messaging channel,
- an incident form,
- a dedicated email address,
- contact with security or the SOC,
- a short intranet procedure.

A report should capture:

- the time of the call,
- the displayed number,
- the claimed identity of the caller,
- the request made,
- actions already performed,
- information disclosed,
- software installed,
- any codes or credentials shared.

Employees do not need to classify the incident themselves.

They only need to communicate the facts quickly.

---

## Shame after the call increases the impact

Someone who disclosed information or installed software may try to hide what happened.

They may fear:

- punishment,
- loss of trust,
- embarrassment,
- professional consequences,
- judgment from colleagues.

The attacker benefits from every minute of delay.

With remote access, they may use that time to:

- browse data,
- compromise accounts,
- perform payments,
- change settings,
- install persistent access,
- contact more people.

The organization should communicate clearly:

> The priority is rapid reporting, not hiding the mistake.

An early response may stop the attack even after the first control has failed.

---

## Stigmatizing the victim creates a false sense of immunity

After hearing about a fraud case, it is easy to say:

> I would never fall for that.

This protects self-esteem but may reduce caution.

People often explain the success of an attack through characteristics of the victim:

- naivety,
- lack of intelligence,
- carelessness,
- age,
- lack of knowledge.

They ignore the context:

- strong stress,
- fatigue,
- loneliness,
- time pressure,
- personal crisis,
- a well-prepared story,
- spoofed caller ID,
- a realistic voice,
- genuine background information.

The belief that only “other people” become victims benefits the attacker.

A safer position is:

> Anyone can make a poor decision under sufficiently convincing conditions, which is why we need processes that interrupt the attack regardless of the person’s emotional state.

---

## Response model for a suspicious call

### Stop the action

Do not disclose information, install software or make a payment.

### End the call

Do not continue a long discussion and do not attempt to prove the fraud to the caller.

### Recover an independent channel

Use the official application, a known number, the corporate directory or a previously saved contact.

### Confirm the situation

Ask the institution whether it attempted to contact you and whether the described event is real.

### Report the incident

Provide the number, time, content of the call and details of any actions already performed.

### Contain the impact

When information was disclosed or software installed, change passwords, disconnect the device from the network and follow the security team’s instructions.

The most important step is interrupting the conversation.

Only then should the facts be established.

---

## Safe rules for telephone conversations

A telephone conversation should never result in disclosing:

- a password,
- an MFA code,
- a BLIK payment code,
- complete card details,
- a PIN,
- a recovery code,
- a remote-session identifier,
- login credentials,
- non-public organizational information.

The recipient should also avoid:

- installing software suggested by the caller,
- disabling protections,
- changing account settings,
- making a transfer,
- sharing the screen,
- opening a link dictated over the telephone.

A legitimate institution should have a process that enables verification without requiring such actions.

---

## Organizational controls that reduce vishing risk

Resilience should combine several layers:

- a clear remote-support process,
- prohibition against sharing passwords and codes,
- mandatory ticket numbers,
- the ability to verify a technician through an internal system,
- approved remote-access tools,
- blocking unapproved remote-administration software,
- restricting user installation privileges,
- an easy reporting channel,
- independent verification of financial changes,
- scenario-based training,
- logging critical operations,
- verification through an official application,
- emergency family or organizational passwords.

Technical and process controls should assume that the caller may sound completely convincing.

---

## Common organizational mistakes

### An informal support process

Employees do not know how legitimate IT support should contact them.

### Authentication based on the telephone number

A saved number is treated as proof of identity.

### No control over software installation

Users can run arbitrary remote-access tools.

### No independent verification of financial changes

A telephone conversation is enough to approve a sensitive operation.

### Reporting is too complicated

Employees do not know where to report a suspicious call.

### Punishing the person who made a mistake

Future incidents are hidden.

### No practical exercises

Employees know the definition of vishing but have not developed the habit of ending the call.

### Excessive trust in voice recognition

A familiar voice is treated as authentication.

---

## Common tester mistakes

### No specific hypothesis

The conversation is impressive but does not test a clearly defined process.

### An excessively emotional pretext

The assessment causes disproportionate stress.

### Continuing after the objective has been achieved

The tester gains remote access even though willingness to provide it has already been proven.

### Using excessive personal information

The scenario becomes unnecessarily intrusive.

### No plan for a real incident

A genuine attack may occur during the campaign and be confused with the simulation.

### Recording without retention rules

The material contains unnecessary personal data.

### Judging the employee instead of the process

The report describes “naivety” rather than the absence of effective verification.

### No safe ending to the conversation

The participant remains convinced that a real incident has taken place.

---

## Vishing campaign checklist

### Preparation

- [ ] Define the hypothesis and objective.
- [ ] Identify the group within scope.
- [ ] Define permitted roles and pretexts.
- [ ] Exclude scenarios that cause disproportionate stress.
- [ ] Define the maximum permitted action.
- [ ] Establish the moment when the call must end.
- [ ] Prepare an escalation contact.
- [ ] Define recording and data-retention rules.
- [ ] Plan for a genuine incident occurring during the campaign.

### Execution

- [ ] Maintain a timestamped log of calls.
- [ ] Do not collect more data than the objective requires.
- [ ] Do not expand the conversation beyond the agreed scenario.
- [ ] Record correct verification attempts.
- [ ] End the assessment after obtaining minimum evidence.
- [ ] Interrupt the call if the participant shows a strong emotional response.
- [ ] Do not involve people outside scope.

### Completion

- [ ] Confirm campaign completion with the coordinator.
- [ ] Delete or secure recordings.
- [ ] Anonymize the report.
- [ ] Describe successful defensive behavior.
- [ ] Present reporting and response times.
- [ ] Explain the influence mechanisms used during the call.
- [ ] Plan a repeat assessment after improvements are implemented.

---

## Reporting model

### Observation

> A person claiming to represent the IT department persuaded a user to download remote-administration software without an existing ticket or independent caller verification.

### Evidence

> Call records, tester notes, the file-download event and confirmation that the application was launched.

### Impact

> A real attacker could gain access to the user’s active session, corporate mailbox, documents and resources available from the device.

### Recommendation

> Implement a consistent remote-support process based on an existing ticket, technician confirmation through an internal channel and blocking unapproved remote-access tools.

The report should show which control was expected to interrupt the attack and why it failed.

---

## One sentence I’m keeping

**Vishing succeeds not because the caller sounds convincing, but because they prevent the victim from interrupting the conversation long enough to verify who is really calling.**
